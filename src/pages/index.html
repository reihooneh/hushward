What usually helps most isn't what costs most

Doors beat ceilings

A closed door stops far more sound than acoustic tiles soak up. Tiles still matter in big hard rooms, where the sound lingers, but they're the expensive way to help a bay off a busy corridor.

Who sleeps where

Beds near the station, the sluice or the bins get the loudest nights. Moving the patients who most need sleep to the quietest beds costs nothing, and the planner finds the best arrangement exactly.

Free fixes first

Moving the night handover, turning the phone down and closing doors cost little or nothing. The chart of what each extra dollar buys shows where spending starts to pay.

How it works it out

  1. Sound to every bed

    Each sound is followed through its room (the direct path, reflections off walls, floor and ceiling, and the lingering echo), out through doorways and along the corridor to every bed.

  2. Chance of waking

    The level at each pillow becomes a chance of waking for that kind of sound, lower for patients who are more easily woken, and adds up over the night.

  3. The best plan

    Every combination of changes is tried, each with the best placing of patients in beds, and the best within your budget is kept, along with the cheapest route to every level of quiet.

The wards and patients here are invented, and the sleep model is an estimate for comparing options. Measure a real ward's sound before changing it, and keep clinical needs, like patients who must be watched closely, first.
